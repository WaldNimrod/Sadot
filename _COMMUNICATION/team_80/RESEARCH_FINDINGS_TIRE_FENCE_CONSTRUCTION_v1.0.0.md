# Recycled-Tire Fence/Screen Construction — Feasibility, Structural, Aesthetic, Code Research

**Client request:** "צמיגים לגדר" (tires for a fence) as part of the west-boundary privacy screen. Client
separately prefers natural stone (kurkar/basalt) over ornamental "moon rock," and has raised a snake/pest concern.

**Confidence key:** 🟢 well-established (multiple independent technical sources) · 🟡 documented variant (real
technique, less universally standardized) · 🔴 plausible/unverified (logical extrapolation, not confirmed)

## 1. Technique inventory — screening/fence use case specifically

| # | Technique | Confidence | Suitability for a boundary SCREEN (not a retaining wall) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | **Rammed-earth-filled tires, stacked in running bond** (classic Earthship/permaculture method) | 🟢 | A tire packed ~95% full of compacted soil is a genuinely dense, self-stable masonry brick — needs no concrete footing or mortar/rebar for basic coursing per Earthship Biotecture's own published guidance. Strongest, most load-tested technique here — **but** documented overwhelmingly as a *retaining* technique (soil berm behind it); freestanding screen use is a real but more constrained case (see below). |
| B | **Tires as stacked planters forming a terrace/screening wall** (unrammed/loosely soil-filled) | 🟢 as terrace/retaining; 🟡 as a tall freestanding screen | Well documented for garden terracing. Loosely filled tires have materially less shear/friction strength than the pounded-earth version and are more prone to shifting under wind load with no soil abutting the far face. |
| C | **Tires as core/void-fill inside a conventional post-and-rail frame** (wired/bolted between engineered posts in concrete footings) | 🟡 | Structurally the *safest* option — actual loads (overturning, wind) carried by conventional post design, well understood and easy to size. Tires are decorative/planting infill, not structure. |
| D | **Tire sidewall "shingle" cladding** | 🔴 | Documented mainly for low-cost roofing in informal/disaster-relief contexts, not vertical garden screens. Weakest-sourced option — not recommended. |

**Key structural distinction:** nearly all the strongest tire-wall literature (Earthship Biotecture, Pangea
Biotecture, permaculture terracing guides) is written for **retaining walls with soil backfill on one side** —
the earth bracing does a large share of the stability work. A **freestanding screen with air on both sides** must
resist overturning entirely through its own weight, footing, and batter. Earthship Biotecture's own technical
page quantifies this explicitly, rating the wall to resist **36 psf equivalent fluid pressure in the
free-standing condition** — even the most rigorous source treats "freestanding" as a distinct, bounded case.
**This is the single most important honesty flag: technique A is genuinely strong and well-tested, but
specifically as a tall, unbermed, freestanding privacy screen it is a narrower, less field-proven application
than its retaining-wall reputation suggests.**

## 2. Height, structural considerations, failure modes

- **Rammed-tire terrace/low wall (technique B):** 0.3–1.2m is routine, low-risk.
- **Freestanding rammed-tire screen (technique A) at "tall privacy screen" height (~1.8–2.2m):** achievable in
  principle, but pushes past the range where standard Earthship literature assumes a soil berm or bond-beam load
  from above — neither applies to a garden boundary screen. Should have:
  - **Footing:** a shallow rubble-trench or lean-concrete strip footing (🟡 recommended add-on for freestanding,
    even though classic bermed Earthship construction often omits one — 🟢 for that different load case).
  - **Batter:** each course stepped back ~2.5–7.5cm from the one below — standard, should not be skipped.
  - **Pinning/rebar:** vertical rebar/bamboo pins through drilled sidewalls tie courses together — a real,
    documented reinforcement variant, not universal in the base technique; recommended especially at ends,
    corners, gate posts.
  - **Fill quality is the biggest variable:** properly pounded/rammed earth (~95% compaction) gives the "brick"
    its rigidity — loosely shoveled soil does NOT deliver equivalent performance. This is the actual difference
    between technique A and B in practice.
- **Failure modes:** (1) tipping/overturning — dominant risk for a tall freestanding run, worse if under-compacted
  or unbattered, and wind load on a tall solid screen is a real design load (the whole point is blocking
  sightlines/wind, which maximizes surface area). (2) UV degradation of exposed rubber in Israel's high-UV summer
  — mitigated by full render/stucco (also solves the aesthetic problem, §3). (3) **Water pooling → mosquito
  breeding + pest/snake harborage** — a well-established, multiply-sourced public-health concern (EPA, US
  agricultural extension, vector-control literature): tires holding standing water are classic breeding sites for
  disease-vector mosquitoes (including West Nile virus vectors, present in Israel) and tire cavities are cited
  insect/rodent harborage — **directly relevant to the client's own stated snake/pest worry.** Fully rammed,
  solid-earth-filled tires (technique A done properly) have no cavity to hold water — the lowest-risk option on
  this dimension. Any technique leaving a tread channel or void hollow and rain-exposed needs drilled weep holes
  as a mandatory detail. (4) Differential settlement/rocking without a uniformly compacted, leveled base course.

## 3. Aesthetic finishing — reconciling with kurkar/basalt, "no moon rock"

The client's material preference is actually well-aligned with the *standard* finish for this construction, not
in tension with it:
- **Full render/plaster coat over the tire face** (earthen plaster or cement stucco, built out over a lath/shaping
  layer) is the standard Earthship-style finish — makes the wall smoother-looking AND solves the UV-exposure
  failure mode. Done well, a rendered tire-core wall is visually indistinguishable from a rendered block or
  rammed-earth wall — no rubber, no "waste tire" reading.
- **Thin natural-stone veneer/cladding (kurkar or basalt) over that render** — adhered stone veneer over a
  rendered substrate is standard, unremarkable landscaping practice; nothing unusual about applying it over a
  rendered tire core instead of CMU or poured concrete (🟡 — the veneer-over-render approach itself is standard
  masonry practice; its specific application over a tire core is a reasonable, low-risk extrapolation, not
  independently documented in the tire-construction sources found).
- **Cheaper alternative:** crushed kurkar/basalt aggregate floated into the final stucco coat (exposed-aggregate
  render) — stone-consistent texture/color without full veneer cost.
- **Planting integration:** climbers or cascading drought-tolerant Mediterranean shrubs at the base/top course is
  the standard permaculture move for softening a stacked-tire wall, pairing naturally with the stone finish and
  reinforcing (not replacing) the screening function.
- **Painting alone** (no render) is a documented low-cost cosmetic option elsewhere but doesn't address UV
  durability as well and reads far more "recycled-tire" than "natural stone" — not recommended given the client's
  stated preference.

## 4. Israeli code/safety considerations (open, not resolved here)

- **National permit-exemption baseline** (תקנות התכנון והבניה (עבודות ומבנים הפטורים מהיתר), תשע"ד-2014): a
  **fence (גדר)** is permit-exempt up to **1.5m** (measured from ground on both sides), not permitted
  street/public-facing without further review, must not damage infrastructure/drainage. A **retaining wall (קיר
  תומך)** is exempt only up to **1m** (local committee discretion up to 1.5m residential / 2m non-residential).
- **Unresolved classification question:** a rammed-earth-filled tire structure has real retaining-wall
  characteristics even when its intended function here is screening, not soil retention. Whether Pardes
  Hanna-Karkur's local committee/outline plan would classify a tall rammed-tire screen as "fence" (1.5m regime) or
  "retaining wall" (1m regime, more likely needing an engineer's stamped drawing) is genuinely open.
- **Boundary-line vs. building-line regime:** this project's own survey already documents קו בניין (building-line)
  setbacks of ~4–7m from various edges — that regime governs habitable/permanent structures set back from the
  boundary, a **different** regulatory question from a fence built directly on the boundary line, typically
  governed by the fence exemption above instead. Where exactly the tire structure sits changes which regime
  applies — needs the actual Pardes Hanna-Karkur local plan (tochnit) or the local vaada, not assumed from
  national defaults.
- **Tire sourcing:** Israel has a dedicated tire law (חוק לסילוק ולמיחזור צמיגים, התשס"ז-2007) placing
  collection/recycling obligations on tire suppliers — relevant context for sourcing via a licensed dealer/
  recycler rather than informal collection.
- **Seismic note (minor positive):** several lay-press sources on rammed-tire retaining walls note resilience to
  impact and ability to flex/recover after seismic events vs. rigid concrete — a possible talking point for a
  seismically active region, though anecdotal/lay-press sourcing (🟡), not engineering-grade seismic data.

## 5. Honesty summary

- **Solid, multi-source-confirmed:** rammed-earth-filled tires in running bond are a genuinely strong, well-tested
  masonry technique — *for retaining/terracing*, the context nearly all this literature actually documents.
- **Real but thinner documentation:** the same technique as a tall, freestanding, unbermed boundary screen — this
  project's actual use case — is structurally plausible (even has a quantified freestanding rating from Earthship
  Biotecture, 36 psf) but is a narrower, less field-proven application than the retaining-wall version.
- **Well-established outside tire-literature specifically:** the mosquito/water-pooling hazard, and the general
  Israeli fence-vs-retaining-wall/building-line distinction (specific classification for a tire structure and the
  Pardes Hanna-Karkur local plan provisions are NOT resolved here).
- **Weakest-sourced, included for completeness only:** tire sidewall shingle cladding — not recommended.

## 6. Open questions / follow-ups for team_00 and a structural-minded contractor

1. Confirm with a local structural engineer/experienced tire-wall builder whether a freestanding screen at
   ~1.8–2.2m needs a poured footing and/or rebar pinning at this site's actual soil conditions.
2. Get a local-plan-specific ruling from the Pardes Hanna-Karkur vaada on classification (fence vs. retaining
   wall) given the structure's exact placement relative to the boundary line and the documented קו בניין
   annotations.
3. Decide technique A (rammed, recommended structurally and on mosquito/pest grounds) vs. technique C (decorative
   infill in a conventional post frame) and get a rough cost/labor comparison — rammed-tire construction is
   labor-intensive (hand/pneumatic tamping of every tire).
4. If technique A: confirm the render + stone-veneer finishing sequence with a plasterer/mason experienced in
   curved/irregular substrates.
5. Source tires through a licensed dealer/recycler for consistent sizing/condition and regulatory cleanliness.
6. If any tire cavities are left hollow/partially open, specify drilled weep/drainage detailing at design stage —
   given the client's explicit pest/snake sensitivity, don't leave this as a site-build afterthought.
