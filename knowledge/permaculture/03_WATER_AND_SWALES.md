## 1. Why water design matters more in a dry-summer Mediterranean climate

Pardes Hanna sits in Israel's coastal-plain Mediterranean climate: essentially **all rain falls in a wet winter
(roughly November–March)**, followed by a **long, completely dry, hot summer** (typically zero meaningful rainfall
June–September). This bimodal extreme — not "moderate rain year-round" — is the core water-design constraint:
the design problem is not "how do we drain excess water" but **"how do we capture and hold the winter water in
the soil/landscape long enough to reduce dry-season irrigation demand."** Every earthwork and planting decision in
this domain should be read against that framing.

Core permaculture water principle (Mollison; also central to Brad Lancaster's *Rainwater Harvesting for Drylands
and Beyond*, a standard reference for exactly this climate type): **slow it, spread it, sink it** — slow water's
overland flow, spread it across more ground area, and sink it into the soil profile — before considering piping
it away. Water that leaves the site as runoff is a resource lost, not a problem solved.

## 2. Core earthwork techniques

| Technique | What it is | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Swale** | A level-bottomed ditch dug exactly on contour, with the excavated soil mounded as a berm on the downhill side | Intercepts overland flow moving downslope, holds it in place, forces infiltration into the soil rather than letting it run off |
| **Berm** | The mounded ridge of soil on a swale's downhill edge (also used standalone) | Planting mound with subsurface moisture reserve from the adjacent swale; slows flow further downhill of the swale itself |
| **Infiltration basin / rain garden** | A shallow, broad depression (not necessarily on contour) that collects concentrated flow (e.g. roof downspout, paved-area runoff) and lets it soak in over hours | Handles point-source water (roof, driveway) rather than sheet flow across open ground |
| **Mulch basin** | A shallow saucer-shaped depression around a single tree/shrub, heavily mulched | Small-scale version of the above, sized to one plant's root zone — the standard technique at residential-garden scale |
| **Check dam** | A small barrier (rock, log) placed across an existing flow channel/gully | Slows channelized flow, reduces erosion, encourages local deposition and infiltration |

**Scale caution for this project:** classic swale-and-berm earthworks (the ditch-and-mound pattern) are most
commonly deployed at orchard/farm scale across open contour lines measured in tens of meters. On a small
residential plot, the same *water-harvesting logic* is usually better expressed through the smaller, denser
techniques above the line in the table — mulch basins at each tree, a rain garden under a downspout, contoured
planting beds — rather than a literal multi-meter swale-and-berm system, which can look and function like a farm
earthwork transplanted awkwardly into a garden. Whether any true swale is appropriate here depends entirely on
the plot's actual slope and area, which is exactly the data that is currently missing (see §4).

## 3. Greywater considerations

Greywater (from showers, sinks, laundry — excluding toilet/blackwater) is a standard permaculture water source in
dry climates, and directly relevant to a private house design:

- **Typical use:** laundry-to-landscape or simple branched drain systems direct greywater to mulch basins around
  established trees/shrubs (never directly onto edible leaves or root vegetables eaten raw, per standard
  greywater-safety practice; sub-surface or mulch-covered delivery only).
  Note: local Israeli plumbing/health regulation for greywater reuse is a real constraint that must be checked
  against actual code before any greywater plan is finalized for this house — this file states general
  permaculture practice only, not a compliance determination.
- **Design implication:** if greywater reuse is adopted, tree/guild placement in `02_GUILDS_AND_PLANTING_STRATEGY.md`
  should preferentially site greywater-tolerant, higher-water-demand guild members (e.g. fruit trees) near the
  house's plumbing exit points, since that is where a gravity-fed or low-pump greywater line is cheapest to run.
- This is a **design option to evaluate**, not a commitment — it depends on the house's actual plumbing layout,
  which is part of the still-pending site data.
