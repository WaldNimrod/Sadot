## 1. The zone model — origin and purpose

Permaculture zone planning (Mollison, *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual*, 1988; Holmgren, *Permaculture:
Principles and Pathways*, 2002) organizes a site by **frequency of human visitation and management intensity**,
not by physical distance alone. The core principle: put what you tend most often closest to where you live, and
let management intensity fall off with distance, so effort is spent where it pays off and low-intervention zones
are left to do their own work.

| Zone | Definition | Typical management intensity |
|---|---|---|
| **Zone 0** | The house / dwelling itself | Daily, highest intensity — kitchen, indoor plants, entryway |
| **Zone 1** | Immediately outside the door | Daily — herb spirals, salad beds, compost caddy, most-visited annual veg |
| **Zone 2** | Intensively managed garden beds, small orchard, chicken coop | Several times a week — perennial veg, dense guilds, small livestock |
| **Zone 3** | Main crop/orchard area, larger-scale production | Weekly to occasional — main orchard, larger annual plots, pasture |
| **Zone 4** | Semi-managed, minimal-intervention forestry/foraging | Occasional — coppice woodland, foraging, extensive grazing |
| **Zone 5** | Unmanaged wild/observed system | Rare, observation-only — reference ecosystem, wildlife corridor |

Zones are not fixed rings of a set radius — their shape follows *actual movement patterns* (a path to the
washing line, a slope, a gate) more than concentric circles. The classic diagram is a teaching simplification;
real zone maps are irregular and site-specific.

## 2. Adapting the model to a small private residential plot

Most zone-planning literature assumes farm or smallholding scale (multiple dunams to hectares), where the full
0–5 range is meaningful. **Sadot is a private house garden on a residential plot in Pardes Hanna** — this changes
the model materially:

- **Zones 0–2 dominate**, and receive almost all of the design attention. Zone 0 (house) and Zone 1 (the
  immediate outdoor-living/kitchen-garden band) will carry the bulk of daily-use planting, seating, and hardscape.
  Zone 2 (any small orchard trees, denser perennial beds) may be the outer edge of the entire usable plot.
- **Zones 3–5 typically do not exist as literal zones on a plot this size.** There is usually no "main
  production field" (Zone 3), no semi-wild forestry belt (Zone 4), and no untouched wild reference zone (Zone 5)
  within a residential boundary. Where a residual "wild edge" exists (an untended boundary strip, a hedge left
  unpruned, an unmowed corner) it can be *treated conceptually* as a miniature Zone 4/5 analogue — valuable for
  biodiversity and low-maintenance ground cover — but it should not be over-designed as if it were a farm's
  extensive-management zone.
- **Practical reframing for this project:** think of the plot less as "which of the 5 zones do we have" and more
  as a **management-intensity gradient inside a single small area** — e.g., "high-touch strip along the kitchen
  door" → "medium-touch perennial/guild beds" → "low-touch boundary planting." The zone *logic* (effort near the
  house, self-sufficiency further out) still holds even when the physical scale compresses zones 2 through 5 into
  a few meters of garden.
- **Vertical + edge intensification compensates for lost area.** On a small plot, techniques that are optional at
  farm scale become load-bearing: trellising and espalier to add vertical zone-1-equivalent growing space,
  guild-stacking (canopy/understory/groundcover/root layers in one bed — see `02_GUILDS_AND_PLANTING_STRATEGY.md`),
  and maximizing edge (the boundary between two different environments, e.g., lawn/bed, sun/shade, wet/dry) since
  edge is where the most productive and diverse plantings tend to sit.

## 3. Sector analysis — the site-specific overlay

Where zones describe *how often you visit*, **sectors** describe *external energies moving through the site*
that the design must work with or against: sun, wind, water flow, views, noise, and access. Sector analysis is
independent of zone placement but constrains it — e.g., a Zone 1 herb bed placed in a cold-wind sector will
underperform regardless of how convenient its location is.

Standard sector categories relevant to this project:

1. **Sun** — path and angle of the sun across seasons; which parts of the plot get full summer sun / winter sun /
   permanent shade; where a deciduous tree could give summer shade + winter light.
2. **Wind** — prevailing wind direction(s) by season (Israel's coastal-plain sites like Pardes Hanna typically see
   a hot dry easterly in transitional seasons and onshore westerlies most of the year); frost-pooling in
   low points on cold clear nights.
3. **Water flow** — where rain naturally sheets or channels across the plot; roof runoff points; any slope
   direction; whether water pools or drains fast (see `03_WATER_AND_SWALES.md`).
4. **Views** — desirable sightlines to preserve or frame (into the garden, out to a landscape feature) and
   undesirable ones to screen (neighboring structures, street).
5. **Noise** — sources of unwanted noise (street, neighboring properties) that a planted buffer could soften, and
   quiet zones worth protecting.
6. **Access** — pedestrian paths, vehicle access, gates, existing hardscape that constrains where beds/trees can
   go without obstruction.
